Rabbi Chayim Yaakov Rottenberg

הרב חיים יעקב רוטנברג

Overview: Rabbi Chaim Yaakov Rottenberg was one of the leading Orthodox rabbinic figures in post-war Western Europe. Born in Poland and raised in Antwerp, he later became Chief Rabbi of the Agudas Hakehilos community in Paris and a central figure in rebuilding Orthodox Jewish life in France after the Holocaust.

Early Life

Chaim Yaakov Rottenberg was born on 14 October 1909 in Wadowice, Poland, into a distinguished rabbinic family. His father, Rabbi Markus (Mordechai) Rottenberg, later became Chief Rabbi of Antwerp, and his mother was Sarah Hendel Friedman.

In 1911, when he was still a child, the family moved to Antwerp, where his father took up the position of rabbi and later became Chief Rabbi of the city in 1918. From that point on, Antwerp became the family’s permanent home base and the center of their communal influence.

He received his early Torah education in Antwerp before continuing his studies in some of the leading Lithuanian yeshivot of the time, including Telšiai, Mir, and Brisk, where he developed a deep foundation in Talmudic analysis and halachic reasoning.

Marriage and Family

On 19 June 1934, Rabbi Rottenberg married Rosa Devorah Rinsky in Antwerp. The couple established their home in the city and had children there.

They had several children, including sons who were born and raised in Antwerp.

During the Holocaust, tragedy struck the family. Rosa Devorah Rottenberg and three of their children were deported from the Drancy transit camp to Auschwitz on Convoy 72 on 29 April 1944, where they were murdered. Only Rabbi Chaim Yaakov Rottenberg survived.

His father, Rabbi Markus Rottenberg, was also murdered in Auschwitz after being deported from Vittel.

Rabbi Rottenberg’s sister, Recha Sternbuch, became one of the most important Jewish rescue activists in Switzerland during the war, helping save thousands of Jews across Europe, though she was unable to save her own immediate family.

Antwerp and Post-War Leadership

After the war, Rabbi Rottenberg returned to Antwerp, which had lost much of its pre-war Jewish population. He became one of the leading rabbinic figures involved in rebuilding Orthodox Jewish life in the city, serving as Rav and Av Beis Din.

During this period, he was deeply involved in restoring communal institutions, strengthening religious education, and reestablishing halachic authority within the devastated community. His leadership in Antwerp positioned him as one of the most respected rabbinic authorities in post-war European Jewry.

Paris and the Agudas Hakehilos

In the early 1960s, Rabbi Rottenberg was invited to succeed Rabbi Samuel Jacob Rubinstein as Chief Rabbi of the historic Rue Pavée Synagogue in Paris and head of the Agudas Hakehilos (Union of Orthodox Communities).

He accepted the position with the understanding that his family would continue his rabbinic legacy, and that one of his sons would later succeed him.

Under his leadership, the Agudas Hakehilos community became one of the most important independent Orthodox Jewish institutions in France, outside the framework of the Consistoire. He was instrumental in strengthening Torah education, expanding kosher supervision systems, and maintaining traditional Jewish life in post-war France.

Customs and Character

Rabbi Rottenberg was known for his humility and strict adherence to traditional Jewish practice. Despite his status, he maintained a modest personal style and was deeply respected for his integrity and scholarship.

At the Rue Pavée Synagogue, he was known for observing specific customs associated with Hasidic tradition, reflecting his Lithuanian-Hasidic background and his commitment to preserving older European rabbinic traditions within a modern French Jewish context.

Death and Burial

Rabbi Chaim Yaakov Rottenberg passed away in Paris on 27 August 1990 (7 Elul 5750). He was buried in the Machsike Hadass cemetery in Putte, Netherlands, alongside other prominent figures of the broader Antwerp Orthodox rabbinic world.

Legacy

Rabbi Rottenberg is remembered as one of the most important Orthodox rabbinic leaders in post-war Europe. His influence spanned Antwerp and Paris, where he played a key role in rebuilding Jewish communal life after the Holocaust.

Through his leadership of Agudas Hakehilos and his earlier role in Antwerp, he helped preserve traditional Torah Judaism in Western Europe at a time of major demographic and cultural upheaval. His legacy continues through his descendants and the institutions he strengthened.
